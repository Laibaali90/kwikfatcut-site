🇯🇲 Jamaica — Comprehensive Food Guide

Jamaican foods
A to Z.

Immerse yourself in Jamaica’s vibrant culinary scene, where every dish celebrates the island’s unique flavours, fresh ingredients, and lively whole-food holistic traditions — offering an authentic taste of paradise. This is not a recipe list. This is an education in what the land provides.

The Purpose

organic, indigenous
whole foods.

This guide honours the nutritional value of local Jamaican produce — fruits, ground provisions, herbs, seafood, and whole grains that have sustained the island’s communities for generations.

Jamaica’s cuisine is lively and authentic, featuring dishes that celebrate the island’s rich culinary Rasta traditions. These traditions align well with the Kwik Fat Cut approach — supporting weight management, encouraging natural skin beauty, and fostering long-term healthy eating habits.

They emphasise whole grains such as wild oats and brown rice, reduce refined carbs, and include iron-rich plant proteins like callaloo and yams. Nuts such as peanuts and pumpkin seeds also fit well within this framework.

Experience Jamaica’s spicy, bold flavours — allspice, pimento, Scotch bonnet peppers, and ginger — that energise and promote overall well-being. Choose steam-grilled options and explore plant-based, whole-food meals wherever possible. Your health is your most valuable asset.

The Full Guide

Ground provisions,
greens, proteins
& whole foods.

Beyond fruits — the full landscape of Jamaican whole foods. Every entry is chosen for its nutritional value, cultural significance, and alignment with the Kwik Fat Cut indigenous food philosophy.

A
National Fruit
Ackee (Blighia sapida)
Jamaica’s national fruit. Originating from West Africa. Rich in omega-3 and omega-6 fatty acids, vitamins A, C, and B-complex, potassium, and flavonoids. Must be fully ripe before consumption. Central to the national dish: ackee and saltfish.
Spice
Allspice (Pimento)
Jamaica produces most of the world’s allspice. Contains eugenol — an anti-inflammatory compound also found in cloves. Used in jerk seasoning, teas, and traditional medicine for digestive complaints.
Fruit
Avocado
Known locally as “pear.” Rich in monounsaturated fats, potassium (more than bananas), and folate. Supports cardiovascular health and nutrient absorption from other foods eaten alongside it.
B
Ground Provision
Breadfruit (Artocarpus altilis)
Introduced to Jamaica in 1793 by Captain Bligh (same voyage that brought ackee). High in complex carbohydrates, fibre, and potassium. Low glycaemic when roasted. A dietary staple that sustained communities through food scarcity.
Fruit
Banana
Jamaica exports Cavendish and plantain varieties. Ripe bananas provide quick energy and potassium. Green bananas act as a prebiotic — resistant starch that feeds beneficial gut bacteria.
Legume
Black-Eyed Peas
High in folate, iron, and plant protein. A staple legume across West Africa and the Caribbean. Used in Hoppin’ John, rice and peas variants, and soups. Supports blood formation and energy metabolism.
C
Leafy Green
Callaloo (Amaranthus viridis)
Jamaica’s most important leafy green. Rich in iron, calcium, vitamins A, C, and K, folate, and antioxidants. Higher in iron than spinach per serving. Used in soups, rice dishes, and as a side vegetable. A nutritional cornerstone of the Ital diet.
Fruit
Coconut
Every part is used — water for electrolytes, flesh for fibre and medium-chain triglycerides (MCTs), oil for cooking and skin health. Coconut water contains naturally occurring cytokinins linked to anti-ageing effects at the cellular level.
Bitter Herb
Cerasee (Momordica charantia)
Known as bitter melon. One of Jamaica’s most trusted bush medicine herbs. Used in teas for blood cleansing, fever, skin conditions, and blood sugar regulation. Contains charantin — documented for hypoglycaemic (blood sugar lowering) activity.
D — F
Ground Provision
Dasheen (Taro)
Corm and leaves both edible. High in complex carbohydrates, potassium, and vitamin E. The leaves are used similarly to callaloo. One of the oldest cultivated food crops in human history — over 10,000 years of agricultural use.
Grass / Herb
Fever Grass (Lemongrass)
Used in bush teas across Jamaica for fever reduction, digestion, and relaxation. Contains citral — an antimicrobial compound. Calms the nervous system and supports healthy sleep. A staple of Jamaican home medicine.
Legume
Gungo Peas (Pigeon Peas)
Essential in Jamaican rice and peas, particularly at Christmas. High in plant protein, folate, iron, and dietary fibre. Supports nitrogen fixation in soil — making gungo peas both a food crop and a soil restorer in traditional farming.
G — J
Root / Spice
Ginger
Jamaica produces some of the world’s finest ginger. Contains gingerols and shogaols — potent anti-inflammatory and antiemetic compounds. Used for nausea, digestion, circulation, and pain relief. Among the most studied medicinal plants on earth.
Fruit
Guinep (Spanish Lime)
Small green fruit with translucent orange-pink flesh. High in vitamin C, iron, phosphorus, and amino acids. Traditional use for anaemia and sleep support. One of Jamaica’s most beloved seasonal fruits — rarely found outside the Caribbean.
Seafood
Jamaican Parrotfish
A reef fish found in Jamaican waters. Rich in omega-3 fatty acids, selenium, and lean protein. Parrotfish play a critical ecological role in reef health — understanding sustainable consumption is part of the Kwik Fat Cut approach to food education.
M — P
Fruit
Mango (East Indian, Julie, Haden)
Jamaica grows over 50 mango varieties. East Indian, Julie, and Haden are among the most prized. Rich in vitamins A, C, B6, and folate. Contains mangiferin — a xanthone with documented anti-inflammatory and antidiabetic properties.
Fruit
Otaheiti Apple (Ethiopian Apple)
Called the Ethiopian Apple in Jamaica. Approximately 91% water — exceptional for hydration. Rich in vitamins A, C, and riboflavin (B2). Low calorie, high in minerals including calcium, iron, and phosphorus. Supports bone health, blood circulation, and overall vitality.
Fruit / Vegetable
Plantain
Eaten at every stage of ripeness for different nutritional profiles. Green plantain: high resistant starch (prebiotic). Yellow: sweet, higher in simple sugars. Ripe: rich in antioxidants. A complete food that adapts across the meal.
S — Y
Pepper
Scotch Bonnet
Among the hottest peppers in the world. Contains capsaicin — documented for pain relief, metabolism boost, and cardiovascular support. Rich in vitamins A and C. Used in Jamaican cooking for flavour, heat, and health simultaneously.
Fruit
Soursop (Graviola)
One of the most studied fruits in natural medicine. Contains annonaceous acetogenins — compounds studied for their effects on cancer cell lines. Rich in vitamin C, B vitamins, and fibre. Used in Ital teas and juices across Jamaica.
Ground Provision
Sweet Potato
Over 102% daily vitamin A from beta-carotene. Prebiotic fibre feeding Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium gut bacteria. Anti-cancer phytochemicals including anthocyanins. A true superfood rooted in Caribbean and African culinary tradition.
Ground Provision
Yam (Yellow Yam, St. Elizabeth Yam)
Distinct from sweet potato — true yams have a lower glycaemic index and different nutrient profile. High in potassium, vitamin C, and B6. Yellow yam from St. Elizabeth parish is among the most prized in Jamaica. Supports hormonal balance through natural diosgenin compounds.

Preparation Matters

How you cook
is as important
as what you cook.

Jamaica’s cuisine offers bold, aromatic options. Choose thoughtfully — maximise nutrition by selecting preparation methods that preserve rather than diminish the food’s natural properties.

♨️

Steam-grilling

Preserves water-soluble vitamins lost in boiling. Maintains natural flavour without the chemical byproducts of high-temperature smoking. The preferred Kwik Fat Cut method for fish and vegetables.

🌿

Slow cooking with herbs

The traditional Jamaican method for peas and beans. Slow cooking with thyme, allspice, and scotch bonnet extracts phytochemicals into the cooking liquid, making the whole dish more nutritious than the sum of its parts.

🥗

Raw & lightly dressed

Callaloo, papaya, and avocado eaten raw or lightly dressed retain the highest concentration of heat-sensitive vitamins. Vitamin C and folate begin to degrade above 70°C.

🫙

Fermentation & pickling

Traditional Jamaican escovitch and pickled vegetables are fermented foods. Fermentation increases nutrient bioavailability and introduces beneficial bacteria that support the gut microbiome.
