The Archive

The great kingdoms and
their food traditions.

West Africa was not a monolithic culture. It was a continent of empires, kingdoms, and city-states — each with distinct food traditions shaped by local ecology, trade networks, and centuries of agricultural refinement. The four great kingdoms most relevant to Jamaica’s food heritage are the Mali Empire, the Songhai Empire, the Benin Kingdom, and the Ashanti Kingdom — all documented through Arab traveller accounts, Portuguese colonial records, and the oral traditions preserved by professional historians called griots.

Mali Empire (1235–1600 CE) — documented by Ibn Battuta (1352) and Ibn Khaldun (1377)

The most documented medieval African food culture

Ibn Battuta — the Moroccan scholar who travelled more widely than Marco Polo — spent eight months in the Mali Empire in 1352 and documented its food culture in precise detail in his Rihla (Travels). He recorded a population eating a diet centred on millet porridge, black-eyed peas, dried fish, fresh fruit, and moringa leaves prepared in multiple ways.

Ibn Battuta noted with admiration the nutritional adequacy and organisation of Malian food systems: granaries were maintained at the village level, food was distributed communally during shortage, and every household maintained kitchen gardens of medicinal herbs. No household went without food — a food security model that modern nations still fail to achieve.

Ibn Khaldun’s Muqaddimah (1377) further documents that West African populations eating millet-based diets were “numerous, healthy, and long-lived” — an observation now confirmed by millet’s documented role in reducing cardiovascular risk and supporting gut microbiome diversity.

Kingdom of Benin (900–1897 CE) — documented through Portuguese records (1485) and British colonial accounts (19th century)

The most sophisticated agricultural kingdom in West Africa

Portuguese explorers who arrived in Benin City in 1485 documented a city larger than Lisbon at the time, with paved roads, organised markets, and a highly productive agricultural hinterland. Their records note extensive cultivation of yams, plantains, palm oil, okra, bitter leaf, and various peppers across a landscape of carefully managed farmland.

The Benin Kingdom maintained a sophisticated system of crop rotation and intercropping that preserved soil fertility across generations — a practice that took European agriculture until the 18th century to formalise through Jethro Tull and the British Agricultural Revolution. Benin farmers were practising it 300 years earlier.

Bitter leaf (Vernonia amygdalina) — a cornerstone of Benin food and medicine — is now documented to contain vernodalin and vernolide, compounds with demonstrated anti-cancer, anti-malarial, and hypoglycaemic properties. The Benin healers who prescribed it understood the outcome. Modern phytochemistry identified the mechanism.

The Diet

What West Africans
actually ate — documented.

Archaeological analysis of ancient West African sites, combined with colonial-era food records and oral traditions preserved by griots, produces a consistent picture of the pre-colonial West African diet. It was plant-dominant, fermented-food-rich, fibre-dense, and extraordinarily diverse — exactly the dietary pattern that modern nutritional science identifies as most protective against chronic disease.

Sorghum
Gluten-free ancient grain. Higher in antioxidants than blueberries. Rich in tannins documented to reduce cancer risk. Supports blood sugar regulation. Drought-resistant — the grain of food security across the Sahel.

West Africa — documented 5,000+ years

Black-Eyed Peas
Iron, folate, plant protein, and fibre. Documented in ancient Egyptian records and West African oral traditions. One of the first legumes carried to the Caribbean. Still a Jamaican staple. Still providing the same nutrients it always did.

West Africa — documented 3,000+ years

Okra (Abelmoschus esculentus)
Soluble fibre that binds cholesterol and supports blood sugar regulation. Its mucilage coats and soothes the intestinal lining. Documented in West African cooking as a thickening agent and medicine for 3,000 years before it appeared in Jamaican pepper pot.

West Africa — documented use across all major kingdoms

Palm Oil (Red)
Rich in beta-carotene and vitamin E. Traditional unrefined red palm oil — distinct from the industrially refined white palm oil — contains carotenoids protective against vitamin A deficiency. Used medicinally across West Africa for skin health and immunity.

West Africa — documented use spanning 2,000+ years

Tamarind
Tartaric acid, antioxidants, and documented antimalarial properties. Used across West Africa for fever management, digestive complaints, and as a vitamin C source. Found in Jamaican cooking to this day.

West Africa — documented in Mali Empire records

Baobab Fruit
More vitamin C than oranges, more calcium than milk, more iron than spinach — per gram. The baobab tree lives up to 3,000 years. Its fruit has sustained West African communities through drought and famine for longer than any written record exists.

What Crossed the Atlantic

Seeds in hair.
Knowledge in memory.
Survival in the hands.

The transatlantic slave trade transported an estimated 12.5 million people from West and Central Africa to the Americas between 1500 and 1900. It is one of history’s greatest crimes. It is also — through the extraordinary resilience of the people it displaced — one of the most significant moments of agricultural and nutritional knowledge transfer in human history.

Historical records, genetic analysis of Caribbean crops, and oral histories documented by scholars including Jessica Harris (author of High on the Hog), Michael Twitty (culinary historian), and Judith Carney (University of California, Black Rice, 2001) establish that enslaved Africans brought specific food knowledge and seeds that transformed Caribbean and American agriculture.

Rice cultivation knowledge — Judith Carney’s groundbreaking 2001 research documented that rice cultivation in South Carolina was possible specifically because enslaved people from rice-growing regions of West Africa brought the technical knowledge to cultivate it. Planters who owned this knowledge, not the planters who owned the land, built the American rice economy.

Okra seeds — Documented in colonial Caribbean records from as early as 1647. Genetic analysis confirms Caribbean okra varieties trace directly to West African cultivars. Okra is now inseparable from Jamaican cooking — in pepper pot, callaloo, and soups.

Black-eyed peas — Brought to the Caribbean and American South as a direct transplant from West African food tradition. Archaeological and documentary evidence places black-eyed peas in Jamaican food culture from the earliest colonial records.

Medicinal herb knowledge — The bush medicine tradition of Jamaica — cerasee, fever grass, search-mi-heart, and dozens of other plants — integrates West African medicinal plant knowledge with Taino plant knowledge. The two traditions, encountering the same Caribbean environment, arrived at complementary rather than contradictory healing systems.

Callaloo — The leafy green that became Jamaica’s most important vegetable is directly descended from West African cooking traditions. The name, the preparation method (sautéed or steamed with aromatics), and the nutritional role it plays are all documented continuations of West African leafy green traditions.

“The enslaved people who were brought to Jamaica did not leave their food knowledge in Africa. They carried it in their hands, in their seeds, and in the memory of every preparation. The food of Jamaica is African food grown in Caribbean soil.”

Food as Medicine

The West African
pharmacopoeia.

West African healing traditions — including Yoruba medicine, Akan healing practices, and the traditions of the Mandinka healers — understood food and medicine as inseparable. Every major healer in West African tradition was first a food knowledge holder. The boundary between nutrition and medicine did not exist.

This understanding produced one of the most extensive plant medicine traditions in the world, documented across hundreds of years of observation and now being systematically validated by clinical research. The following represent West African food-medicines whose traditional uses have been confirmed by modern science.

Bitter leaf (Vernonia amygdalina) — Documented across Igbo, Yoruba, and Efik healing traditions for malaria, diabetes, and fever. 2012 research in the Asian Pacific Journal of Tropical Biomedicine confirmed significant anti-malarial activity in bitter leaf extract. Charantin compounds demonstrate blood sugar lowering effects comparable to some pharmaceutical interventions.

African basil (Ocimum gratissimum) — Used across West Africa for respiratory infections, fevers, and skin conditions. Laboratory analysis has identified eugenol, thymol, and carvacrol — documented antimicrobial compounds that inhibit bacterial growth. The traditional use as a respiratory medicine is consistent with documented anti-inflammatory effects on airway tissue.

Moringa oleifera — Called the “tree of life” across West Africa for over 2,000 documented years. Every part is edible and medicinal. Modern analysis confirms it contains 92 nutrients, 46 antioxidants, and 36 anti-inflammatory compounds — making it among the most nutritionally dense plants ever documented. The traditional claims made about it are not exaggerated.

Ginger — Introduced to West Africa via ancient trade routes from Asia and fully integrated into West African food medicine within centuries. Documented across Mali, Benin, and Ashanti traditions as a digestive, anti-inflammatory, and fever remedy. Over 150 clinical studies now confirm ginger’s efficacy for nausea, inflammation, and blood sugar regulation.

The Miracle Tree

Moringa —
what 2,000 years of West African knowledge knew.

Moringa oleifera has been used in West African food and medicine for over 2,000 documented years. The Ben Cao Gang Mu (Chinese Compendium of Materia Medica, 1596) also documents moringa, confirming its independent discovery and use across multiple ancient civilisations. This convergence across unconnected cultures is the strongest possible evidence of genuine nutritional significance.

What one serving of moringa leaves (21g, fresh) provides:

Protein: 2g — Vitamin B6: 19% of daily value — Vitamin C: 12% of daily value — Iron: 11% of daily value — Riboflavin (B2): 11% of daily value — Vitamin A: 9% of daily value — Magnesium: 8% of daily value. It also contains all nine essential amino acids — making it one of the few plant-based complete proteins. West African mothers have fed moringa to infants and nursing women for two thousand years. Modern nutritional science took until the 1990s to document why this was correct.

The National Institutes of Health (NIH) describes moringa as “almost every part of the moringa tree has been used as food” and notes its documented anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and neuroprotective properties. The NIH is confirming what West African grandmothers already knew.

Jamaica’s Green Foundation

Callaloo —
Africa in a pot.

Callaloo (Amaranthus viridis) is the most nutritionally significant leafy green in Jamaican cuisine. Its cultivation and preparation methods trace directly to West African leafy green traditions — particularly the use of leafy amaranth species across Nigeria, Ghana, and Senegal, where it has been eaten for over 3,000 documented years.

The word “callaloo” itself is documented in Jamaican records from the 17th century, shortly after the period of largest African agricultural knowledge transfer. It is an African name for an African vegetable grown in Caribbean soil.

Iron content: Callaloo contains approximately 3mg of iron per 100g — comparable to beef and significantly higher than spinach when adjusted for bioavailability with vitamin C. West African populations relying on plant-based iron sources developed cooking traditions (pairing iron-rich greens with acidic fruits) that maximise absorption. This is not coincidence. It is nutritional intelligence refined over generations.

Calcium: 266mg per 100g — higher than cow’s milk per serving. The West African populations that consumed minimal dairy maintained excellent bone density through callaloo, moringa, and sesame-based calcium intake. Archaeological bone density analysis of West African skeletal remains confirms this.

Folate: Critical for neurotransmitter synthesis, DNA repair, and the prevention of neural tube defects. Callaloo is among the richest plant sources available in the Caribbean. West African food traditions that emphasised callaloo consumption during pregnancy were providing folate supplementation two millennia before the supplement existed.

The Living Legacy

West Africa in
every Jamaican kitchen.

The food of Jamaica is the food of West Africa, grown in Caribbean soil, combined with Taino knowledge, and refined across centuries of community practice. When a Jamaican grandmother makes pepper pot, she is preparing a dish whose roots extend to the kingdoms of Benin and Mali. When callaloo is sautéed with onion and scotch bonnet, the technique traces to West African leafy green preparation methods documented in 17th century colonial records.

This heritage is not simply cultural pride — though it deserves that too. It is a nutritional instruction manual: a record of what combination of foods sustained large, healthy, long-lived populations for thousands of years before chronic disease became the dominant global health threat.

Returning to real food in Jamaica means, in large part, returning to Africa. Not as a cultural rejection of the present, but as a nutritional homecoming — to the foods the body was shaped to receive, prepared by the methods that preserve their greatest value, eaten in the community that amplifies their benefit.
