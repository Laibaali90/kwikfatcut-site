The Foundation

A superfood
celebrated across time.

The sweet potato is a high nutrient density “superfood”, celebrated across cultures for its vitamins A, C, fibre, and antioxidants. Historically used in traditional medicine to treat digestive issues including diarrhoea, constipation, indigestion, and bloating — the sweet potato was understood as medicine long before science gave it a profile.

In Jamaica and across the Caribbean, sweet potato is a ground provision — a staple of the ancestral diet that has sustained communities for generations. It is not an exotic health food. It is home food. Modern nutrition science can now describe what our grandmothers worked out by feeding people well.

“High nutrient density makes sweet potatoes a superfood — celebrated for vitamins A, C, fibre, and antioxidants that the body can actually use, meal after meal.”

Vitamin A is critical for good eyesight, immune defences, and organ function. Sweet potato is one of the richest everyday sources of beta-carotene, about 8,500 micrograms per 100g raw and about 11,500 micrograms baked in the skin, which puts it alongside carrots and well ahead of spinach or kale. The body converts beta-carotene to vitamin A as it needs it. Unlike preformed vitamin A from animal foods and supplements, beta-carotene from food is not known to cause vitamin A toxicity. Very high intakes can turn the skin a harmless yellow orange, which fades when intake drops.

Digestion

A prebiotic that
feeds your gut.

Sweet potatoes contain high levels of dietary fibre, acting as a prebiotic that promotes beneficial gut bacteria — specifically Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium. These bacteria are among the most studied and health-supportive in the human microbiome.

A baked medium sweet potato provides about 3.8g of dietary fibre. Fibre adds bulk and is fermented by bacteria in the large intestine. Laboratory studies using sweet potato fibre have shown it can be fermented by gut bacteria including Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium, though that work has been done in test tubes and in animals rather than in people. What is well established is simpler: eating more fibre from whole plant foods supports normal bowel function.

Sweet potato has a long history in traditional medicine for digestive complaints including diarrhoea, constipation, indigestion and bloating. These traditional uses have not been tested in clinical trials, so we cannot say that science has confirmed them. What we can say is that sweet potato is a good source of fibre, and that fibre supports normal digestion.

Immunity

Boosting the body’s
natural defences.

The immune-supporting profile of sweet potato is multi-layered. Vitamin A maintains the integrity of mucosal surfaces — the body’s first-line physical barriers against pathogens. Vitamin C supports the production and function of white blood cells. Together, they create a comprehensive immune defence from a single whole food.

Beyond vitamins, sweet potatoes contain an array of phytonutrients — compounds that plants produce for their own defence, but which benefit human health when consumed. These are compounds that cannot be replicated in a supplement because they work synergistically with each other and with the food matrix they come from.

Research

What laboratory
research is looking at.

Purple fleshed sweet potato varieties are rich in anthocyanins, the pigments that give purple and red produce their colour. In laboratory work, isolated sweet potato anthocyanins have slowed the growth of cancer cells grown in dishes, including breast, stomach and colon cell lines. This is early stage laboratory research. It has not been tested in people, and results in cells and animals often do not carry over to humans.

The National Cancer Institute notes that randomised trials of antioxidant supplements have not shown a benefit for preventing cancer, and explains why laboratory results often do not transfer to people. It is also worth knowing that most Caribbean sweet potato is white, cream or orange fleshed and contains little anthocyanin, so this research is not about the sweet potato most of us eat. Nothing here means sweet potato prevents or treats cancer, and no food does. Eat it as part of a varied diet built on vegetables, fruit and whole foods. That pattern, rather than any single food, is what the evidence supports.

Phytonutrients

The compounds
science is still learning.

Phytonutrients are the frontier of nutritional science — compounds identified in the last few decades that traditional and ancestral diets have delivered for millennia. Sweet potato is rich in all of them.

Beta-carotene
Precursor to vitamin A. Antioxidant. Orange pigment — the deeper the colour, the higher the concentration.
Lutein
Protects the eyes from oxidative damage. Associated with reduced risk of macular degeneration and cataracts.
Zeaxanthin
Works with lutein for eye health. Also has anti-inflammatory properties throughout the body.
Anthocyanins
Predominantly in purple fleshed varieties. Studied in the laboratory for antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity.
Flavonoids
Broad class of anti-inflammatory, antioxidant compounds that protect blood vessels and support cardiovascular health.
Tannins
Support gut health and have antimicrobial properties. Work synergistically with fibre to support the intestinal barrier.
