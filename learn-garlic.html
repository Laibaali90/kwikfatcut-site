Five Civilisations. One Truth.

Valued for
thousands of years.

Scientifically known as Allium sativum, garlic is a bulbous plant with cloves encased in papery skins, belonging to the Allium family alongside Welsh onions, shallots, leeks, chives, and Chinese onions. Native to the Black Sea region, it now grows worldwide in temperate areas with rich, well-drained soils.

But the story of garlic is not botanical. It is a story of convergence. Five of the greatest civilisations in human history, working independently across thousands of years, all arrived at the same conclusion about this plant.

Ancient Egypt
The Builder’s Food

Garlic appears in the Ebers Papyrus, an Egyptian medical text from around 1550 BC, among the remedies recorded for a range of complaints. Tradition holds that the labourers building the pyramids were given garlic to keep up their strength, though that reaches us as a long standing story rather than as a documented record. It was both food and pharmacy.

Ancient Greece
The Athlete’s Herb

Greek Olympians consumed garlic before competition for stamina and endurance. Hippocrates — the father of Western medicine — prescribed it for pulmonary complaints, parasites, and digestive disorders. He called it a “natural cleanser.”

Ancient Rome
The Soldier’s Companion

Garlic was eaten by Roman soldiers and sailors, who valued it for strength and endurance on campaign. Dioscorides, a Greek physician who served with the Roman army, wrote that garlic cleans the arteries, and recorded its use for bites and for the chest. Ancient writers did not mean by that what a doctor would mean today. These accounts show how highly garlic has been valued for a very long time. They are not evidence of what it does in the body.

Traditional Chinese Medicine
The Blood Mover

Chinese medicine has used garlic for over 3,000 years. Classical texts describe its role in moving blood, warming the stomach, and detoxifying the body. It was prescribed for digestive complaints, respiratory infections, and worms — all conditions now confirmed by modern research.

Ayurvedic Medicine — India
The Rejuvenator

Ayurvedic texts classify garlic as a rasayana — a rejuvenating substance that promotes longevity. It was prescribed for heart disease, arthritis, digestive weakness, and as a general tonic. Allicin, garlic’s primary active compound, was not isolated by science until the 20th century. Ayurveda understood its effects for millennia before that.

Caribbean & African Tradition
The Kitchen Healer

In Jamaican and West African cooking traditions, garlic has always been more than flavour. Used in bush medicine preparations for fever, infection, and digestive complaints — it arrived in the Caribbean through the same culinary traditions that carried so much ancestral wisdom across the Atlantic. The kitchen was the pharmacy.

“Five civilisations. Thousands of years. One convergence. Garlic is not a health trend. It is the most documented medicinal food in human history.”

The Two Types

Softneck & hardneck —
what’s the difference?

There are two main types of garlic, softneck and hardneck, differing in flavour, storage ability, and growing habits. Understanding the difference helps you choose and use garlic most effectively.

Softneck Garlic

The most common type. Flexible stem, multiple layers of cloves, longer shelf life. Milder flavour. This is the variety typically found in supermarkets. Suited to warmer climates. Braided for storage.

Hardneck Garlic

More complex flavour. Stiff central stem, fewer but larger cloves, shorter shelf life. Preferred by cooks and herbalists for its intensity. Grows best in cooler climates. Higher allicin content in many varieties.

Immune Support

Garlic and
the immune system.

Garlic’s immune-boosting properties are primarily driven by allicin — a sulphur compound released when garlic is crushed or chopped. Allicin has documented antimicrobial activity against bacteria, viruses, and fungi, including strains resistant to conventional antibiotics.

One small clinical trial, 146 adults over 12 weeks, found that people taking a daily garlic supplement reported fewer colds than people taking a dummy capsule. That is a single study, and its author was affiliated with a commercial garlic organisation. The Cochrane review of garlic for the common cold looked at all the available research and concluded there is not enough evidence to say whether garlic prevents or treats colds. In that same trial, once a cold had started it took about as long to clear in both groups. Garlic is a good food to eat every day. It is not a proven cold remedy.

Allicin — antimicrobial against bacteria, viruses, fungi. Activated by crushing or chopping. Destroyed by high heat — use raw or lightly cooked for maximum effect.

Ajoene — anti-clotting compound that improves blood fluidity and reduces risk of cardiovascular events.

Quercetin — anti-inflammatory flavonoid that modulates immune responses and reduces histamine release.

Selenium & manganese — trace minerals that support antioxidant enzyme production, protecting cells from oxidative stress.

Cardiovascular Health

Blood pressure &
the heart.

Garlic is among the better studied foods for blood pressure. When researchers pool the randomised trials, garlic supplements lower systolic blood pressure by roughly 5 to 8 mmHg on average in people whose blood pressure is already raised, with a smaller effect on diastolic pressure. That is a real but modest change, the trials are small, and the researchers who run these analyses say larger and better designed studies are still needed. Garlic has not been shown to prevent heart attacks or strokes, which is what blood pressure medication is prescribed to do, so it is an addition to good care and not a replacement for it.

The mechanism involves garlic’s ability to increase nitric oxide production in blood vessel walls. Nitric oxide relaxes the smooth muscle lining of arteries, widening the vessels and reducing resistance — the same fundamental mechanism targeted by many blood pressure medications.

Cholesterol

A cholesterol-
lowering agent.

Garlic supplements may lower total and LDL (“bad”) cholesterol by a small amount in people whose cholesterol is already raised. Pooled trial evidence found falls of roughly 17 mg/dL in total cholesterol and 9 mg/dL in LDL after more than two months of use, with HDL (“good”) cholesterol barely moving. The evidence is not consistent. A carefully run six month trial at Stanford gave 192 adults raw garlic, a powdered supplement or an aged garlic extract, and found no effect on LDL cholesterol at all.

Before you take garlic supplements

Garlic supplements are not a replacement for prescribed medication, and they are not free of side effects. They can cause breath and body odour, stomach pain, wind, nausea and allergic reactions, and they can increase the risk of bleeding. If you take warfarin or another blood thinner, aspirin, medication for blood pressure or cholesterol, or HIV medication, or if you are due to have surgery, speak to your doctor or pharmacist before taking garlic supplements. Never stop or reduce a prescribed medication in favour of a food or a supplement. Garlic in cooking, in normal amounts, is a different matter and is fine for most people.

How garlic might affect cholesterol is still not settled. Animal feeding studies have reported changes in several of the enzymes the body uses to make cholesterol, and laboratory work on human liver cells points mainly to an enzyme called squalene monooxygenase rather than to HMG-CoA reductase, the enzyme that statin medicines block. None of this has been confirmed in people, so garlic should not be described as working like a statin.

The Bigger Picture

Community health
through education.

This overview highlights garlic’s role in community health through education and collective effort. Researchers are still studying garlic, and some questions remain open. What is already clear is that garlic is a flavourful, inexpensive food with a long history and a modest, well tolerated place in a healthy diet.

Not a supplement. Not an extract. The whole clove, grown from the earth, prepared with care. Exactly as every civilisation that understood health has always recommended.

Humble root.
Extraordinary legacy.

Garlic may modestly support blood pressure and cholesterol, and its long history across Egypt, Greece, Rome, China, India, and the Caribbean makes it a humble and worthwhile food. Crush it. Chop it. Let it rest for ten minutes before cooking to maximise allicin activation. Eat it often. Understand what it is doing for you, and what it is not.
